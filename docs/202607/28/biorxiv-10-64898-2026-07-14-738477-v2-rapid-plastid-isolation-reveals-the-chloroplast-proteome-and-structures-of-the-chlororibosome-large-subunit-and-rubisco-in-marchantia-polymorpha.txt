Abstract 17 Plastids house the biology of eukaryotic photosynthesis. While 1000s of plastid genomes have been 18 sequenced, the availability of less than ten proteomes and only two species with full 70S plastid ribosomal 19 structures limit our understanding of plant evolution. We optimized a protocol for the rapid isolation of 20 

Marchantia polymorpha plastids that provides a highly enriched and intact organelle fraction from gradient 21 volumes as little as 2 mL. The approach was successfully applied to six other species. Focusing on 22 

M. polymorpha , we determined the proteome of the plastid fraction, identifying 1337 nuclear-encoded 23 proteins with a high confidence, where 83% belong to orthologs shared with angiosperms. We further 24 isolated protein complexes by RNA affinity purification using poly-lysine and provide the high-resolution 25 structures of the 50S subunit of the chloroplast ribosome and RuBisCO using cryogenic EM and image 26 reconstruction to 2.23 and 2.12 Å resolution, respectively. For plastids, our data show that the genome 27 reduction event experienced by the bryophyte common ancestor has had little impact on the organelle’s 28 complexity and they underscore a high level of structural conservation of key components. Our data provide 29 novel resources to explore the functional evolution of plastid proteomes and major macromolecular 30 complexes of cyanobacterial origin. 31 32

Introduction 34 The transition from a cyanobacterial endosymbiont to the different types of plastids of algae and plants 35 entails the loss and transfer of most of the endosymbiont’s genetic information 1–5 . Crop plants such as pea 36 or maize hence encode more than a thousand proteins in their nuclear genome that need to be imported by 37 their plastids after cytosolic translation 6,7 . Plastid proteomes are complemented by a few proteins that are 38 kept organelle-encoded, maybe due to the need for the co-localisation of genes of the electron transport 39 chain and the bioenergetic membrane itself for functional redox regulation 8–10 , and protein hydrophobicity 40 impairing membrane translocation 11–14 . Subunits required during the early steps of 70S ribosome assembly 41 are likely retained due to constraints associated with securing proper ribosome maturation 15 . Typically, as 42 is the case for the 70S ribosome, plastid-encoded proteins interact with nuclear-encoded ones to form 43 macromolecular complexes such as photosystem I and II, the NADH dehydrogenase complex, the ribulose-44 1,5-bisphosphate-carboxylase/-oxygenase (RuBisCO) enzyme, and ATP synthase. 45 A significant event in plastid evolution was the transition from water to land 16,17 . Plant terrestrialization and 46 the origin of the Embryophyta is characterised by the emergence of the cuticle, stomata, spores and seeds, 47 multiplastidy, the adaptation of stress response networks already present in the streptopyhte algal ancestor, 48 a step-wise expansion of genomes, and a general increase in mitochondrial and plastid proteome size 17–23 .49 An early-branching and ecologically important group of land plants are the bryophytes, uniting liverworts, 50 hornworts, and mosses (Fig. 1). After genome expansions at the origin of the Chloroplastida and again at 51 the origin of Streptophyta 24,25 , the bryophyte ancestor experienced genome reduction that drove the 52 lineage’s phenotypic evolution 19 . Now, after more than 500 million years, bryophytes are characterized by 53 highly derived genomes that in sum encode a larger variety of gene families than their vascular relatives 26 .54 The positioning of bryophytes on the phylogenetic tree provides an ideal opportunity to explore the 55 trajectory of plastid biology after genome reduction in comparison to vascular plant plastids. 56 Experimentally curated plastid proteomes and high-resolution structures of macromolecular complexes 57 outside of a few angiosperm model systems, however, are scarce (Fig. 1). To address this, we optimized a 58 chloroplast isolation protocol that requires minimal resources and time. It yields intact chloroplasts from a 59 diverse set of species and eases their study, enabling chloroplast biology exploration across a broad variety 60 of taxa. Focusing on the liverwort Marchantia polymorpha, we obtained a highly enriched chloroplast 61 proteome, which corroborates previously predicted evolutionary trajectories of land plant plastid 62 proteomes 20 . With the aim of isolating chlororibosomes, we subjected the plastid fraction to an RNA affinity 63 purification using poly-lysine 27 . This resulted in the enrichment of multiple macromolecular complexes 64 from the isolated chloroplasts. Using cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), we resolved the structures 65 of the 50S ribosomal subunit and RuBisCO at respective resolutions of 2.23 and 2.12 Å. Our data provide 66 new resources to study plastid biology and allow us to hypothesize that the chloroplast proteome and 67 ribosome of the bryophyte were not significantly affected by genome reformatting at the origin of the 68 bryophytes. 69 70

Lineage-specific gene family expansions contribute to the M. polymorpha plastid proteome 131 Comparative proteomics is an important component in understanding organelle evolution and the 132 contribution of the compartment to the ecophysiology of a plant. Ancestral state reconstruction of 133 mitochondrial and plastid proteomes across a billion years of chloroplastidal evolution recently underscored 134 the expansion of organelle proteomes from algae to angiosperms 20 . It predicted an intermediate chloroplast 135 proteome size for M. polymorpha and we find that 85.6% of the experimentally-curated nuclear encoded 136 chloroplast proteins were predicted based on orthology with previously experimentally characterized 137 proteins (Fig. 3b). Apart from corroborating the identity of individual chloroplast proteins, it also shows 138 that an orthology-based approach to predict an organelle proteome has merit for plotting broader 139 evolutionary patterns in the absence of an experimentally-curated proteome. Hence, we clustered the 140 genomes of 57 diverse chloroplastidal species (Table S3) into orthogroups following the approach used 141 previously 20 and focused on 4,797 plastid orthogroups (POGs). The structures of the 50S ribosomal subunit and RuBisCO demonstrate a high level of 179 

macromolecular complex conservation 180 

With the aim of determining a chlororibosome structure for M. polymorpha , we subjected the purified 181 organelles to RNA affinity purification by poly-lysine (RAPPL) 27 . The eluate of this purification was used 182 for mass spectrometry, which showed an enrichment of all chlororibosome subunits, the ribulose-1,5-183 bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO) enzyme, and proteins of PSII, PSI, and the ATP synthase 184 (Table S4). The eluate was used for cryo-EM grid preparation and subsequent single-particle reconstruction. 185 2D classification revealed several large protein complexes including recognizable views of the 186 chlororibosomal large 50S subunit (cLSU), RuBisCO, the soluble F1-ATPase complex, and a chaperone 187 (Fig. S4). Of these, there were sufficient varied views of RuBisCO and the 50S for three-dimensional 188 reconstruction. No particle classes containing the full 70S or the small 30S ribosomal subunit were 189 identified. 190 We reconstructed high-resolution density maps of the cLSU and RuBisCO to respective resolutions of 2.23 191 and 2.12 Å, reported at the 0.143 Fourier shell correlation cut-off 51 (Fig. 4a & 5a, S4-5). These maps were 192 used for atomic modeling using previously published sequences 52–58 . The ribosomal protein (RP) structures 193 were first predicted using AlphaFold3 41 and then fitted and refined into the experimental map. The rRNA 194 was mutated from a Spinacia oleracea model (PDB ID 5MLC 59 ). The quality of the density map allowed 195 for modeling 6 rRNA modifications in the cLSU 23S rRNA chain (Fig. S6). Out of the 6 modifications, 5 196 were identified previously in Chlamydomonas and Escherichia coli structures, whereas the remaining 197 modification, U579 to 5-methyluridine, appears to be novel to Marchantia (Fig. S6) 60–62 . Alignment of the 198 spinach 50S to the Marchantia 50S revealed only 5 subunits out of 31 with a root mean square deviation 199 (RMSD) above 3.0 Å. These were L5, L13, L21, L33, and PSRP6, with RMSDs of 3.7, 8.1, 3.1, 3.2, and 200 4.5 Å respectively. The values reported for L5 and L33 are due to variation in the position of a single small 201 loop region in each. The main difference between PSRP6’s across the structures is that 9 more C-terminal 202 residues were modeled in the Marchantia model, however these residues are also present in the spinach 203 PSRP6 sequence, so this represents a difference in flexibility or map quality. The main noticeable difference 204 between the Marchantia and spinach models was found at the surface near the exit tunnel, where RPs L13, 205 L21, and L22 are present (Fig. 4b). In S. oleracea , these RPs have regions at their N- and C-termini that 206 stick out from the surface and they were hypothesized to form an interface with the thylakoid membrane 59 .207 Comparisons of structure and sequence suggest that the C-terminal region was extended (e.g. L21) or gained 208 (e.g. in L22) by the ancestor of eudicots, since they are absent or shorter in other chloroplastidal species, 209 including M. polymorpha (Fig. 4b). The N-terminal sequence of L13 is present in the annotation of the 210 Marchantia protein but does not appear in the density map (Fig. 4b). Overall, while the amino acid 211 sequences for chloroplastic RPs between the S. oleracea and M. polymorpha 50S share on average 68% 212 identity, they exhibit highly similar structures through conserved folds (Table S5). 213

Here, we have developed a rapid and efficient plastid purification protocol that is effective across six tested 252 plant lineages. By combining our plastid isolation approach with a RAPPL protocol to enrich chloroplast 253 protein complexes, we provide high-resolution structures of the large subunit of the plastid ribosome and 254 RuBisCO from M. polymorpha . Together with the plastid proteome, our high-resolution structures of two 255 key protein complexes present a unique window into land plant evolution, as bryophytes are characterized 256 by an ancient genome reduction event and a subsequent gene family expansion independent from that of 257 vascular plants 19,26,72 .258 The oldest fossil record of a plastid is that of the filamentous red alga Rafatazmia chitrakootensis . Their 259 rhodoplasts were captured in 1.6-billion-year-old rock formations of the Tirohan Dolomites from central 260 India 38 . At that point in time, these red algae (rhodophyte) plastids had already evolutionarily diverged from 261 those of their common ancestor of Archaeplastida, from which the Glaucophyta and Chloroplastida (the 262 green lineage) also evolved 73 (Fig. 1). Ever since, the photosynthetic organelles of algae and plants have 263 experienced constant adaptations, with the transition to a terrestrial habitat unequivocally representing a 264 groundbreaking step for the evolution of life. 265 Bryophytes occupy an evaluative position in the evolution of land plants. They are the sister-clade to all 266 vascular land plants 72,74 , have retained ancestral features such as flagellated sperms or a dominant, 267 photosynthetic haploid gametophyte 75–77 , and their genomes have independently diverged from those of 268 vascular plants after having been reduced in their common ancestor 19,26 . We find the Marchantia chloroplast 269 proteome to comprise just over 1300 proteins, of which more than 80% are represented by orthologs in 270 angiosperms. The large number of orthologs speaks for a trade-off between ancient nuclear genome 271 reduction and the need to retain – or even expand – gene families needed for the complexity of plastid 272 biology that had to adapt to a terrestrial habitat. We predict that additional organelle proteomes from 273 bryophytes will support this pattern. 274 Annotating the M. polymorpha plastid proteome finds almost 200 proteins of unknown function, with only 275 sporadic domains being identifiable in some cases. TPR and PPR proteins were particularly enriched. TPR 276 domains are key for protein-protein interactions and are involved in chloroplast chlorophyl biogenesis, 277 photosystem assembly and intracellular chloroplast distribution 78–81 . PPR domains are crucial for plastid 278 RNA editing and organelle-targeted members of this protein family expanded with the origin and 279 diversification of land plants, probably to compensate for an increase in mutations caused by higher levels 280 of UV radiation 20,82–85 . Theory has it that complex thalloid liverworts such as M. polymorpha lack mRNA-281 editing possibly due to secondary loss 86,87 , but recent results on Cyathodium cavernum revealed a minimum 282 of 129 canonical C-to-U mRNA editing sites, suggesting that this is not a universal trait of the taxon 88 .283 Studies thus far describe on average 100 proteins with PPR (or related RNA processing) domains encoded 284 by liverwort genomes 89,90 , though their intracellular localization was not clear. We find 35 proteins 285 (including 22 with unclear functions) with such domains among the chloroplast proteome of M. polymorpha 286 – a species that evidently does not edit its plastid-encoded genes in the same way that C. cavernum does. 287 This serves as a reminder that studying diversity often challenges generalizations and it raises the question 288 why dozens of PPR domain-containing proteins are then imported. The RAPPL proteome does contain 300 proteins of the small subunit of the chlororibosome (Table S4) and, in combination with the absence of 301 additional assembly factors on the cLSU structure, it suggests the 70S chlororibosome disintegrated during 302 preparation. 303 Several ribosomal proteins such as L2 to L6, L14, and L16 are among the very few reliable phylogenetic 304 markers across the diversity of life 105 . The main reasons are two-fold: their conserved core structure over 305 4 billion years of evolution and an almost strict vertical inheritance even in prokaryotes 63,106–108 . The 306 bryophyte’s chlororibosome shares this overall level of structural conservation, but there are noticeable 307 differences concerning the absence of terminal loops in L13, L22 and L23 when compared to the spinach 308 chlororibosome (Fig. 4). The extended loops in L22 and L23 appear eudicot-specific, however, and are 309 hypothesized to form an interface with the thylakoid membrane 59 . Their functional relevance in eudicots 310 remains to be demonstrated, along with proteins that might functionally replace these regions in the 311 liverwort. The absence of the L13 N-terminal extension in the density map – while being present in the 312 annotated gene – implies either a high degree of flexibility, disorder or potentially a post-translational 313 cleavage event. Whatever the reason, this region of the bryophyte’s chlororibosome seems to better 314 resemble its ancestral roots than that of its eudicot relatives on land. 315 RuBisCO takes a special place in evolution. It is likely the most abundant protein structure on Earth and 316 the vast majority of biomass owes its origin to it, yet it remains one of the least efficient enzymes we know 317 109–111 . It also appears resistant to change. Its macromolecular structure has not been noticeably modified 318 across the vast diversity of photosynthetic eukaryotes since its origin as a hetero-oligomeric complex in 319 cyanobacteria some 3 billion years ago 106 (Fig. 5). The evolution of RuBisCO witnessed the rise of O 2, a 320 molecule that lowers its efficiency by several orders of magnitude. Despite a strong selection pressure for 321 its active center to exclude oxygen, RuBisCO remained unaffected and, instead, distinct carbon capture 322 mechanisms evolved 110 . When introduced into E. coli , not a single nucleotide mutation was observed after 323 150 chemostat generations for the two genes encoding RuBisCO, despite the prokaryote’s biochemistry 324 mutationally adapting to use CO 2 as its sole source for synthesizing hexose, pentose and triose sugars 325 instead 112 . Thus, RuBisCO presents us with an extreme case of an enzyme that is part of a functional 326 module, in which proteins with many interaction partners or those at the center of critical metabolic fluxes, 327 change in sequence significantly slower than their partners 113,114 . The enzyme’s resilience to environmental 328 changes is telling and might explain why its biotechnological optimization remains kind of a Sisyphean 329 task. 330 The combination of our plastid isolation and the RAPPL protocol, together with cryogenic EM offers a 331 powerful toolset for exploring the structural proteome of large, soluble protein complexes of the plastid 332 stroma. The application of this workflow in a single species illuminates how plastid proteomes have evolved 333

Plant growth conditions 343 

We used ecotype Takaragaire-1 (Tak-1) of the liverwort Marchantia polymorpha throughout the study. The 344 wild type gemmae were grown under continuous light (70 μmol m -2 s-1 ) on half-strength Gamborg B5 345 vitamin agar-plates (GVA). 346 

Plastid isolation 347 

The scaled-down and rapid plastid method applicable to a wide-range of species rests on a protocol 348 published by Eftychis Frangedakis 115 . The plant tissues were lysed using mortar and pestle, in the presence 349 of 5-10 mL isolation buffer (IB; 50 mM HEPES KOH pH 7.5, 0.33 M sorbitol, 1 mM MgCl 2, 1 mM MnCl 2,350 2 mM EDTA pH 8, 0.5% w/v BSA, 0.5% w/v PVP40, and freshly added 5 mM ascorbate). The lysate was 351 filtered through a pre-soaked (in IB) Mira cloth and centrifuged at 1000 g, 10 min. The pellet was 352 resuspended in 200 μL IB and loaded onto a gradient of 70% Percoll ® (400 μL at the bottom of the tube) 353 and 30% Percoll ® (1 mL overlayed). Density gradient centrifugation was conducted at 4300 g, 30 min (in 354 a swing bucket rotor). Broken plastids were gently removed first from the top of the 30% Percoll ® layer. 355 Intact plastids were harvested from the 70-30 layer and washed with 10 mL wash buffer (WB; IB without 356 BSA and PVP40) by centrifugation at 3000 g, 10 min. The plastid pellet was washed again with 10 mL WB 357 by centrifugation at 2000 g, 10 min. The pellet was washed one final time with 1 mL WB by centrifugation 358 at 1000 g, 10 min. From the plastid pellets, crude lysate was formed by pulverizing them in presence of 359 liquid nitrogen. Protein isolation buffer (Invitrogen TM , Catalogue number FNN0011) was added to the 360 powder and was incubated at 50 °C for 15 min for resuspension. The debris were pelleted at 10,000 g, 10 361 min and the supernatant was used for western blotting. 362

Comparative organelle proteomics 418 

We clustered 56 Chloroplastida genomes and sorted them into organelle orthogroups as previously 419 described 20 . Briefly, we downloaded whole genome models for diverse Chloroplastida species, clustered 420 them into orthologous protein families (or orthogroups) using OrthoFinder version 2.5.4 118 and annotated a 421 subset of these orthogroups as plastid orthogroups (POGs) based on the presence of experimentally 422 validated plastid proteins from species where available: A. thaliana 36 , maize 35 , rice 35 ,423 

Physcomitrium patens 34 and C. reinhardtii 32,33 , as well as M. polymorpha (current study). Through this 424 approach, more than 99% of ca. 15k experimentally validated plastid proteins were efficiently clustered 425 into ca. 5k POGs (Table S3). The high efficiency is likely explained by the added experimental proteomes 426 and removed non-chloroplastidal species, where homologues are more diverged. 427
